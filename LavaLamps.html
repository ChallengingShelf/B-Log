The internet is protected by lava lamps

A write-up on Cloudflare's obscure yet effective lava lamp wall encryption method, highlighting strength in randomisation.

HamiltonHacks October 2026 3 min read

Here's why a lava lamp is a more secure encryption method. Wtf? I promise it's not a clickbait title this time.

Encryption aims to encrypt using random numbers, but computers are actually never truly random. Instead they are pseudo-random, meaning that numbers may look random but are ultimately defined in a logical and deterministic way, and so they could be reproduced if this deterministic way is known. Y'know?

So how do we get truly random data?

Well, the real, non-digital world is actually pretty random and unpredictable. One way is using lava lamps, and this gets some bonus points because it looks cool as f*ck whilst being super secure.

The way that this works is that in the Cloudflare San Francisco office there is a wall of 100 lava lamps with a camera pointed at them tracking their movements. These images of the lava lamps' shapes and blobs are stored as a series of numbers (every single pixel has its own number value assigned to it), meaning that each picture of a lava lamp becomes a truly completely random number which can then be used to create secure encryption keys. Because the movements are truly random, then turned into images with matching numbers, the numbers used for the encryption are truly random too.

The wall of lava lamps in Cloudflare's San Francisco office

And so, to generate encryption keys for SSL/TLS encryption, Cloudflare uses these lava lamps and their corresponding photo data to feed a cryptographically secure pseudorandom number generator. I will let Cloudflare themselves explain much better than I can in the following two articles:

  • https://www.cloudflare.com/en-gb/learning/ssl/lava-lamp-encryption/
  • https://blog.cloudflare.com/harnessing-office-chaos/

Nothing new under the sun (or the lamp)

Strength in complete randomisation isn't new, this is just such a great example. The idea was actually originally patented by Silicon Graphics as Lavarand way back in 1996. You can read about the expired patent here: https://patents.google.com/patent/US5732138

Pendulums, mobiles and wave machines

In addition to the lava lamp random movement tracker, Cloudflare have actually created some similar, interesting alternative methods with that same goal of strength in randomisation:

  • London - a wall of many, many double pendulums whose swings generate more random movement.
  • Austin - a series of hanging translucent mobiles, which actually move when the doors in the office open and close, and with the sunlight throughout the day.
  • Lisbon - a series of small wave machines working as their source of entropy. It's actually the cover image for this write-up and I think it looks fantastic.

Naturally, I have written this as a high-level overview of how this works, but if you want a deeper dive on the technical details of each method I would refer you to the Cloudflare tech blogs for each project.

Keeping the internet safe and looking cool doing it. Vibes + Security.
